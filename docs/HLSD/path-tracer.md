# Path Tracer

## Purpose

The interactive GPU path tracer provides physically based progressive rendering in both the 3D model view (Prepare) and the sliced toolpath preview (Preview). It visualizes material optics, realistic lighting, soft contact shadows, surface gloss, and volumetric transmission through translucent 3D printing filaments. When enabled in application preferences (`SETTING_OPENGL_PATHTRACE`), the path tracer renders continuously in the background during camera stillness and converges progressively across frames, providing accurate depth and material perception without stalling interactive navigation.

Quality and performance are controlled by a single preference (`SETTING_OPENGL_PATHTRACE_QUALITY`) with three presets:
- **Low:** 64 max samples, 1 indirect bounce, 4 max transmission bounces, 4 shadow hits, 8.0 ms tile budget, 15–50% dynamic orbit resolution, no subsurface scattering.
- **Medium (Default):** 128 max samples, 2 indirect bounces (1 in Preview, where bounce rays start inside dense toolpaths, run incoherent and cost several times a camera ray, while the second bounce adds about half a percent of brightness), 8 max transmission bounces, 8 shadow hits, 10.0 ms tile budget, 18–65% dynamic orbit resolution, lateral diffusion and thin-wall backlight.
- **High:** 256 max samples, 2 indirect bounces, 12 max transmission bounces, 8 shadow hits, 12.0 ms tile budget, 20–75% dynamic orbit resolution, lateral diffusion and thin-wall backlight.

## Views of the rasterizer

The path tracer draws the objects itself, and the rasterizer draws them afterwards for their depth only, so that gizmos, modifiers and the selection are hidden behind them. What the object shaders of the rasterizer add to the surfaces is therefore drawn again or rebuilt:

- The painting gizmos, the variable layer height editor and X-Ray draw the objects through their own shaders, which is the point of these views: the path tracer is off while they are open (`_is_pathtrace_enabled`).
- The overhang view, the darkening of the parts of an object outside the print volume and the band between the printable heights of two extruders are warnings that stay on while working, so the path tracer applies them as `gouraud.fs` does, on the surfaces the camera sees (`object_view_tint` in `pathtrace.comp`). The flags of an object, partly outside the print volume or a wipe tower, ride in its instance. `_update_volumes_state` computes the print volume and the outside state before the path tracer runs.
- The depth pass marks the selection in the stencil, so that `SelectionOutline` outlines it over the image as in the plain view. The sinking contours are drawn in a second depth pass with the color writes turned on for them (`ColorWritesOn` in `3DScene.cpp`). The rasterizer hides them where an object is in front by drawing the objects over them, which a depth only pass cannot; the second pass tests them against the depth of all objects instead.

## Acceleration structure (TLAS / BLAS)

Scene geometry is organized in a two-level bounding volume hierarchy consisting of a top-level acceleration structure (TLAS) over instance bounds and bottom-level acceleration structures (BLAS) for individual meshes or toolpath groups. Modifiers and unprintable parts are no part of the print: they stay out of the scene, and the rasterizer draws them over the path traced image as in the plain view.

- **BLAS (Bottom-Level BVH):** Built asynchronously using a Surface Area Heuristic (SAH) with binned splitting on a background worker thread (`_build_pathtrace_scene_async`). Node data is packed into 32-byte GPU nodes (`PathTraceGpuNode`) flattened in breadth-first order so that the two children of each inner node occupy contiguous array indices (`[child, child + 1]`). BLAS building accepts an atomic cancellation token (`cancel_token`) to abort promptly when the camera or scene geometry changes.
- **TLAS (Top-Level BVH):** Constructed on the main thread over instance world-space axis-aligned bounding boxes (AABBs). In scenes with four or fewer instances, rays test instances directly with minimal overhead; in scenes with more than four instances, a binary top-down bounding volume hierarchy (`TlasBuffer`, binding 4) is traversed logarithmically with a fixed-size traversal stack and distance-ordered child visiting.
- **Identity Transform Fast-Path:** Instances whose transform is an identity matrix (marked via instance metadata) bypass local ray transformation and normal transformation, reducing register pressure and memory bandwidth in both primary and shadow traversal.
- **Deferred Normal Reconstruction:** Triangle intersection records only the closest hit primitive index without evaluating edge cross-products or vertex transformations. Geometry normals and edge-length logic are reconstructed once per ray after traversal completes.
- **Shading Normals:** Meshes in Prepare carry vertex normals per triangle (`NormalBuffer`, binding 5), each octahedron encoded in 32 bits. At a corner they average the faces around the vertex that bend from the corner's face by less than 30 degrees, so curved surfaces shade round while the edges of a part stay sharp. They follow the Smooth normals preference, as the rasterized views do: without it every triangle shades flat. Rays leaving the front of a smooth triangle start from the point lifted onto the surface its vertex normals describe (Hanika, "Hacking the Shadow Terminator"), so that shadow terminators follow that surface instead of the facets. Toolpaths in Preview are no triangles but analytic beads, whose normals are exact.
- **Buffer Layout:** Geometry is uploaded to OpenGL Shader Storage Buffer Objects (SSBOs):
  - Binding 0: Node buffer (`NodeBuffer`)
  - Binding 1: Triangle buffer (`TriangleBuffer`), which holds the beads of a bead instance too
  - Binding 2: Instance buffer (`InstanceBuffer`) with local-to-world and world-to-local transformations; the w of its minimum bound is -2 for the bed, 1 for a part or seam markers and 2 for the toolpath beads
  - Binding 3: Shading metadata (`ShadingBuffer`) containing linear albedos, layer heights, and extrusion roles
  - Binding 4: Top-level acceleration structure (`TlasBuffer`)
  - Binding 5: Vertex normals of smooth triangles (`NormalBuffer`), Prepare only

Transformations of objects or plates update only the instance buffer without triggering a BLAS rebuild. In Prepare there is one BLAS per object instance, built relative to the instance transformation; each BLAS knows its instance by `GLVolume::geometry_id.second`, the ID of the `ModelInstance`, and not by object and instance indices. An arrange or an undo can reorder the objects of the model, and the canvas then keeps its volumes and renumbers them, so the meshes and the BLAS stay while the indices change. The explosion of the Assemble view moves parts within an instance and so rebuilds the BLAS. Color changes in Prepare or Preview update vertex shading buffers in-place via `glBufferSubData` without rebuilding either hierarchy, triggering triangle buffer uploads only when opacity values change.

## Frame scheduling and interactivity

To preserve high UI responsiveness (≥ 60 FPS) during scene interaction and convergence:
- **Interactive Pass:** While the camera or the scene changes, and as the first frame after any reset, a single reduced-resolution pass (`is_interactive_pass = true`) runs the same path tracer as the progressive passes, so the image keeps its look while orbiting: one sample through pixel centers, the key light from the center of its disk, one indirect bounce, a lower cap on indirect contributions, and the denoiser at full strength.
- **Persistent Images:** All images are allocated at the full render size (at most 1440 pixels on the long side) and only follow the canvas size. The interactive pass renders into their top left corner and has its own display images, sampled with sub-UVs in the composite; it stays on screen until the first full-resolution sample is complete, so releasing the mouse never reallocates textures or stalls on a full frame in one dispatch.
- **Interleaved Tile Scheduler:** From sample 1 onward, progressive accumulation dispatches fixed 128×128 tiles within a dynamic GPU frame budget (8–12 ms), which leaves the rest of a 60 Hz frame to the rasterizer and the UI. Tiles are dispatched in a quasi-random Golden-Ratio order ($(\text{cursor} \cdot \text{stride}) \pmod{\text{total\_tiles}}$) that distributes compute load evenly across sky and dense object geometry, preventing latency spikes. The display is only resolved when a sample completes, so it never shows tiles at different sample counts.
- **Dynamic Orbit Scaling:** During active camera rotation or dragging, the interactive resolution follows an 8 ms GPU target (`m_pathtrace_dynamic_orbit_scale`). The cost of a frame follows its pixel count, so the scale meeting the target follows from the scale and time of the measured frame: the controller drops straight to it and grows back gradually.
- **Interactive Reprojection:** Each interactive frame projects the surface point of every pixel into the previous interactive frame and, where that pixel saw the same surface (same class of surface and albedo, similar normal, same distance within the footprint of a pixel on that surface, as a tolerance relative to the distance would span many extrusions and smear their shading along the camera motion) or the background there too, blends its sample with the history kept there, over at most 6 frames: few enough for the shading to follow the camera bound key light. The history is read from the nearest pixel, not interpolated, which would blur the image every frame and blend what lies behind translucent surfaces in neighboring pixels. It is a copy of the last interactive frame's accumulation, bed term, first surfaces and their class, dropped when anything but the camera changes. Each frame takes a new jittered sample, and the denoiser filters with a narrower luminance edge stop once samples accumulate.
- **ImGui Decoupling & Scene Caching:** Active ImGui widget adjustments (layer slider, palette controls) suspend compute dispatches and blit the existing display image to maintain maximum UI framerates. Once `max_samples` is reached, `GLCanvas3D::_is_scene_cacheable()` activates `SceneCache`, reducing idle GPU usage to 0%.

## G-code toolpaths

In Preview, every extrusion segment is one analytic primitive, a bead (`PathTraceBead`), instead of a mesh:

- **Shape:** the section is a stadium, width by height: a box of (width - height) by height between two half cylinders of radius height / 2, the shape of an extrusion pressed onto the layer below. It is swept from the start of the segment to its end, both at mid height of the layer, and turned around the vertical axis at both ends, so that the ends are round from above and from the side. In the bead's frame (x along the segment, y across it, z up) it is the set of points within the radius of the flat region a disk of radius (width - height) / 2 sweeps along the segment. Consecutive beads overlap at their joint, so a turn is the union of two beads, as in a real extrusion, and the grooves between neighboring lines and layers are real geometry: they occlude, shadow and reflect without any shading trick.
- **Storage:** a bead takes the 48 bytes of a triangle: its start and end where a triangle has its first two vertices, the half width of its flat top and its radius in the third. Its bounds are tight for any slope of its segment (`path_trace_bead_bounds()`), and the binned SAH builder, which works on the bounds of primitives, builds the BVH over them. A bead test costs several triangle tests, so the bead BVH puts one bead in a leaf where the triangle BVH puts up to four: the deeper tree culls more beads than its extra boxes cost. The beads have an instance of their own, flagged in the instance buffer, so that a leaf holds either beads or triangles and the test a traversal runs is the same for a whole instance. Seam markers are triangles in their own instance; the bed is triangles too.
- **Intersection:** the bead is convex, so a ray crosses it in one interval, and the distance from its flat region is convex along the ray. Newton's method on that distance, started where the ray enters the bead's box in its frame, therefore converges monotonically to the surface and never steps through it: in one step on the flat top and bottom, in a few on the round sides and ends; started where the ray leaves the box, going back, it finds the exit. It stops within 1e-4 mm of the surface; at most 12 steps are taken, and a ray that has not converged then, a graze, misses. Misses take the most steps, so a ray whose line passes the section of the bead's body on one side is rejected before them. Closed forms for the body, with Newton's method left for the ends, render the same image but run slower on the GPU: the rays of a warp split between their branches, where the plain loop keeps them together. The ray is taken relative to the bead's start, which keeps the precision of beads far from the origin. The normal is the gradient of that distance at the hit, exact everywhere. `intersect_path_trace_bead()` in `PathTracer.cpp` mirrors the shader's `bead_interval()` for the tests.
- **Inside:** rays leave opaque beads from outside, so their closest hit is where they enter a bead. Translucent beads, the section view, which cuts beads open, and the probe looking for the back of a thin wall also see where a ray from inside leaves a bead, as a back face.
- **Fused translucent beads:** overlapping translucent beads are one body to camera paths: a face inside another bead is no surface. The closest hit of a ray can only lie inside a bead the ray started in, since the entry of any other bead would be closer, so the traversal only keeps where the beads around the ray's start end, and the path steps over such a hit without shading it. Where the ray enters the body it takes half the path through the first bead, and where it leaves the rest of the way between the two; only these faces shade, scatter and take a shadow ray. Otherwise the faces and overlaps inside a print would show as a net of lines and rings. The path steps over one hit at a time, as seam markers and opaque beads may lie further inside. Shadow rays still take each bead they cross on its own: they meet beads in any order, and fusing them would take a sorted walk along the ray.
- **Overlaps:** neighboring lines of a layer overlap, a top surface over its wall for one, and share the plane of their flat tops, where a ray hits both at the same distance. Hits within 1 µm of each other are one, and the bead printed later takes it, as it lies on top in the print; the traversal looks that far past the closest hit to find it. Otherwise whichever bead the traversal met first would win, pixel by pixel, and the edge between the two would fray.
- **Joints and slopes:** collinear segments of the same feature, width and height are merged into one bead, and long diagonal ones split, so that the boxes of the BVH stay small.
- **Debug mesh:** for comparison, a checkbox in the FPS overlay of the path traced Preview (`SETTING_OPENGL_PATHTRACE_TOOLPATH_BEADS`) traces the toolpaths as the triangle mesh the beads replace instead: diamond sections for outer walls, upper bevels for inner walls and sparse infill, flat ribbons with rounded ends for the other features, whose shading normal the shader rebuilds as a round bead from the facet kind and where the hit lies on the facet (`toolpath_shading_normal()`), with darkened grooves and a translucent wall estimated from the layer height (`bead_path()`). Both kinds live in the shader at once, each in its own instance; switching rebuilds the BVH.
- **Ironing** overlaps its own lines many times; their flat tops make one flat surface, which lies a hair above the top surface it irons, so that the two never fight where they are level.
- Seam markers are diamond octahedra exempt from shadow casting and subsurface diffusion. With beads they reach a line width out and a layer height up and down: at the flank of a bead, half the width out, they are half a layer high, so that the markers of the layers of a wall join up into a band.
- Height and move clipping (`z_min`, `z_max`, `move_min`, `move_max`) cull primitives both during ray-box intersection tests and inside leaf intersection routines.
- The layers around the ones the sliders show are greyed or dimmed as in the rasterized preview: libvgcode's `LayerShading` gives the rule its toolpath shaders apply, and the path tracer applies it to the toolpaths the camera sees, by the top of their layer, which the beads carry. Greying replaces the albedo with the rasterizer's grey. Dimming is a view aid, not a material: it darkens the whole surface, highlights included, by the linear equivalent of the rasterizer's 8 bit sRGB scale, since on dark filaments the highlights would otherwise make most of the brightness and hide it. Light reaching other parts from a dimmed layer is not dimmed.

## Lighting

The light rig matches the rasterizer's shading model, so that a path traced filament has the color the rasterizer gives it: a white Lambertian surface facing the key light takes 0.70 of irradiance (divided by π) from it, 0.22 from the fill light, and 0.19 + 0.05·n.z from the sky.

- **Key light:** a disk of 0.045 rad, sampled only by next event estimation. BSDF-sampled rays never hit it, so each light has exactly one estimator and needs no multiple importance sampling. It follows the camera unless static shadows are selected.
- **Fill light:** directional and unshadowed, evaluated through the BSDF at every surface. It stands for a large soft box, so its highlight is never sharper than a GGX alpha of 0.35.
- **Sky:** a dome whose radiance rises linearly with the height of the direction, $L = 0.19 + 0.075\,z$, so that its irradiance has the closed form above. Rays that escape see it; paths that end at their bounce limit, and the interactive pass, take its analytic irradiance instead of sampling it.
- **Bed:** rasterized, so the path tracer only sees it in reflections and indirect light, with a dark albedo like the default plates: a bright one would make objects mirror a bed the viewer cannot see. Seen from below, where the rasterizer draws the bed see-through, the path tracer leaves it out, so that the print shows through it.

## Materials

Every filament is a dielectric of index 1.5 (exact Fresnel) with a GGX microfacet interface, sampled from its visible normals. The roughness comes from the shading parameters; after the first bounce it is raised to a floor (path space regularization), which removes most caustic fireflies.

Filament colors are those the rasterized views show, through `adjust_color_for_rendering()`: near black lifted to a grey, the more the more translucent, and an opacity of at least 10%. libvgcode gets them adjusted for Preview, and the path tracer adjusts them itself in Prepare, so that a filament looks the same in both.

The Glossy toolpaths preference means in Preview what it means in the rasterized views. Off, translucent toolpaths are matte and opaque ones get a soft, broad highlight, their GGX alpha raised to 0.5, instead of the glossy plastic one.

- **Opaque filament:** a Lambertian base under the GGX coat, the diffuse part scaled by the Fresnel transmission in and out and normalized to reflect exactly the albedo at normal incidence. One direction is sampled from the mixture of the two lobes and weighted by the mixture pdf.
- **Subsurface scattering of opaque filament:** Burley's normalized diffusion, with a mean free path of 0.12, 0.35 or 0.8 mm by SSS mode (at most 0.35 mm for toolpaths) and per-channel diffusion lengths from the albedo. On camera-visible surfaces a radius is importance sampled from the profile of a random channel, projected on the tangent plane, and the surface found there decides the visibility of the key light; the cosine keeps the normal of the visible point, so layer lines and bead shapes stay crisp while shadow edges soften and take the color of the deeper channels. It is skipped when the diffusion length is below half a pixel. On the medium and high presets a surface lit from behind also takes the light diffusing through a thin wall of its own part.
- **Translucent filament:** the opacity of a filament color is the share of light 0.4 mm of it does not let straight through (see [translucent-filaments.md](translucent-filaments.md)). The pigment and haze diffuse that share in the filament color, as an opaque filament does: half back and half on through a clear wall, all of it back from an opaque one, so the look runs continuously from a clear tinted filament to the opaque one. The light going straight through takes the color of the dye, about four walls of it bringing white light to the hue of the color, so thick parts saturate.
  - Printed walls are thin slabs with near parallel faces, so light leaves them in the direction it came in: each crossing of a surface takes the Fresnel reflection of the interface and the absorption and scattering of a wall. A print is no solid block of plastic: it neither bends the view through it nor reflects totally inside, so the path tracer does not refract. In Preview only the first toolpath wall a path crosses reflects: behind it, beads lie fused to their neighbors, plastic against plastic, where nothing reflects, and their reflections of the sky would lay a haze over everything inside a translucent print.
  - In Preview the wall is half the path length of the ray's line through the bead, which the intersection gives exactly: a bead is crossed twice, at its front and its back.
  - In Prepare a part is closed and stands for a print: each crossing passes one 0.4 mm wall, and the inside is sparse infill of which 1.5% is plastic. A ray knows it was inside when it next hits a back face, so no medium stack is kept.
- **Layers of opaque filament:** with the "Light through filament layers" preference, in the Preview views that color toolpaths by filament, an opaque filament is no longer opaque at the thickness of a layer. Before the first surface a camera ray hits is shaded, a ray from there walks the beads behind it and adds them up as Kubelka-Munk layers, by the length of its line through each along the normal of the surface. It takes a new direction, cosine distributed into the plastic, for each sample, as light inside it is diffuse: a straight ray would stay in its own layer on a side wall, and the layers would stay stripes. What the beads reflect together becomes the albedo of that surface, which is then shaded as any opaque one. It differs from sample to sample, so the denoiser and the reprojection go by the filament of the surface. A layer reflects the filament color when thick, so a thick stack keeps the exact color, and its luminance is down to 1.5% at the filament's transmission distance, estimated from the brightness of its color. That gives one scattering value for all channels, so the channels a filament reflects little of die out much sooner, up to a limit that keeps the pale tints of a thin layer: the layers filter each other's light as pigments do, and alternating yellow and blue make green, as mixed filaments intend. An average of what the layers reflect would make them grey. The layers are added up in the reflectance inside the plastic, converted by Saunderson's correction for the surface of an index of 1.5, and air behind the last layer sends back what that surface reflects inside. Air wider than the grooves between beads ends the walk: the light going on through it is scattered and lost in the dark of the part, and what lies across it, as infill or the far wall, would otherwise show through as sharp lines. Colors are mixed per RGB channel: the true spectra of filaments are unknown, and on the project this was tested with, smooth spectra upsampled from the colors came no closer to the mixed filament colors of `FilamentMixer` than RGB did. The layers are added up rather than scattered between, as most of the light between thin layers goes to and fro many times: paths of a few bounces would lose it and turn a stack of white layers grey. This keeps the cost on the camera ray, and shadow rays and bounces still end at opaque beads. So light does not spread sideways between the layers and no light comes through from behind the print. The walk stops when less than 2% gets through, or after 32 layers, 12 in interactive frames, where what lies behind counts as more of the last filament.
- **Seam markers** are half lit and half self-illuminated, so they stay readable, and cast no shadow.

## Shadows

Shadow rays towards the key light take the transmittance of the translucent primitives they cross, in any order: the Fresnel transmission of each interface and the transmittance of the wall behind it, half the path length through the bead in Preview, at each of the bead's faces the ray crosses, or one wall in Prepare, which is the dye-tinted light going straight through plus the diffused light leaving on the far side in the filament color. In Prepare they also take the Beer-Lambert attenuation of the infill, summed with the sign of each triangle's facing so that the terms of a closed part leave the length inside it. An opaque triangle ends the ray, as does the walls' transmittance falling below 1%; past the shadow hit budget of the preset the partial transmittance is kept.

## Sampling and robustness

- **Sample sequence:** each pixel draws hash-based Owen-scrambled Sobol points (Burley 2020). Every 2D dimension pair shuffles the sample index with its own seed, which decorrelates pairs across bounces and pixels while keeping each pair stratified over the samples of a pixel.
- **Pixel filter:** the camera ray of a sample is offset from the pixel center by a Gaussian of 0.45 pixels (filter importance sampling), so the accumulation filters the image with that Gaussian rather than a box over the pixel. A box lets through the frequencies around the pixel pitch, and the layer lines of a print seen from afar fold back from there into moire rings however many samples accumulate; the Gaussian damps them for a barely softer image.
- **Ray origins** are offset off the surface by a few ulps along the geometric normal, on the side the ray leaves (Wächter and Binder), and hit points are rebuilt from barycentrics, so the offset holds at any coordinate magnitude and ray length. A direction below the geometric surface ends the path.
- **Kernel structure:** GLSL inlines every call, so each call site of a traversal adds a full copy of it to the kernel, and the register pressure of those copies slows every ray, taken or not. The path loop therefore traces one ray per iteration from a single call site, and first the one shadow ray the previous vertex queued; a vertex that needs a subsurface probe ray parks its continuation until the probe is answered. Small scenes and the top level BVH share one call site of the bottom level traversal, whose stack holds node indices only. Shadow rays visit the nearer child first too: in dense toolpaths the occluder is mostly a neighbor bead, which that finds at once. Subsurface probes only traverse their own part.
- **Shadow rays through translucent walls** cannot stop at the first hit and must cross every wall up to the light, through every box of the BVH on their way: in translucent toolpaths they make most of the cost. They go through a Russian roulette: those of highlights on walls behind the first wall a path crosses, dimmed by the walls in front, are traced a quarter of the time, and those of bounced light half the time, at the inverse weight. The weight applies after the firefly cap, which would otherwise cut the scaled contributions more often and darken the image.
- **Variance control:** Russian roulette on the throughput after each bounce, a luminance cap on indirect contributions, and a sample cap before accumulation; samples with NaN or infinite values are dropped.

## Denoising and compositing

The path tracer accumulates, per pixel, the radiance of everything but the rasterized bed and background with the coverage of the path, and the bed term: the ratio of the light reaching the bed to that of an unoccluded bed, times the transmittance of the translucent parts in front of it. It also keeps the running average of the first surface's normal, distance, albedo and class (background, bed, opaque, translucent).

`pathtrace_denoise.comp` resolves the accumulation into the display images whenever a sample completes. Three a-trous passes (steps 1, 2, 4; a step of 8 leaves blocks around sharp highlights) filter the radiance divided by the albedo of opaque surfaces, with edge stops on normal, distance, albedo, class and luminance, and filter the bed term the same way. The luminance edge stop of the radiance follows the variance of each pixel's average, as in SVGF: the path tracer keeps the sum of the squared luminance of the samples (`moment_image`), the first pass takes the variance of a 3x3 neighborhood, and each pass carries it on. Where a pixel is still noisy, neighbors of a different luminance blend in; where it has converged, its edges and texture stay, so the filter limits itself and is not blended out. On a test scene this halves the error to a 1024 sample reference at 16 and 64 samples, where a filter fading with the sample count blurred converged edges. The bed term keeps no variance: its filter is blended over the unfiltered one with a weight of $\min(1, 4/\sqrt{n})$ for $n$ samples. Interactive frames do not keep the variance either, and the first progressive sample has none: a single sample has no spread, so its variance is zero and the filter would leave the noise as it is. Both are filtered at full strength, with a wide luminance edge stop for a single sample.

The tone curve is the identity up to 0.8, so lit filament colors stay exact; above it, it compresses highlights on the largest channel and scales the color by the same factor, which keeps the hue of bright saturated filaments, and light far above white runs towards white, as on film. Filmic curves such as AgX also bend the colors below white and bleach every lit surface, which a slicer showing filament colors cannot afford. The composite first multiplies the rasterized bed by the bed term (stored halved in RGBA8 and blended with `GL_DST_COLOR, GL_SRC_COLOR`), then draws the path traced image over it, preserving bed markings inside colored and ambient shadows.
